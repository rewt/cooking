## The Narrative

I spent the last few months being a worse teammate than I am a professional.

I wasn't the lead on the project. I've been the lead plenty of times, and somewhere in the back of my head I'd quietly decided that made the role I *was* given beneath me. That decision leaked out everywhere. I was short in meetings. There was an edge in my messages. I pushed on work I thought was important instead of the work that was actually on the board. Nobody did anything to me. I just didn't like the seat.

Then I watched Jeff Teague tell a story about Derek Fisher.

Rookie Teague talks trash to Fisher. Calls him washed. Flames the headband. Fisher — a grown man with five rings — turns around and sons him on the spot. And the reason the clip lands isn't the trash talk. It's that Teague, years later, tells it on himself. He was wrong, and he says so.

Because here's what a rookie can't see yet: Derek Fisher was never the best player on any of those teams. Not close. He played 18 years next to Kobe and Shaq by knowing exactly what he was there for and doing that one thing at an elite level, on time, every time.

**The NBA doesn't have a talent problem. It has a role problem.**

Every single person in that league was the best player in their gym, their city, their school. All stars. All leaders. And most of that superior talent still has to go find a role that someone else defined for them. The guys who do it get rings. The guys who won't get highlights.

Consulting is the same building with better coffee.

Every senior person on a project team was the lead somewhere else. That's *why* they're on the team. But the project doesn't need five leads — it needs the deliverable shipped, the timeline hit, the client confident, and exactly one person calling it. If that person isn't you, your seniority isn't a grievance. It's a resource.

So what does leading without the title actually look like?

- **Find the real requirement, not the org chart.** Ask the lead what they're worried about at 11pm. That's your role. It's rarely the one in the SOW.
- **Make the lead's job smaller, not louder.** Bring them a decision, not a debate.
- **Raise the concern once, clearly, in the right room — then commit.** Disagreeing twice is input. Disagreeing five times is sabotage with good intentions.
- **Cover the gap nobody's claimed.** There's always one. Taking it is the fastest way to become load-bearing.
- **Be the person whose piece never gets checked twice.** That's the whole reputation.
- **Give the credit away.** It compounds. It always comes back.

The hard part of this post is the part I have to own: the version of me that was frustrated wasn't a leader being underutilized. It was an ego being inconvenienced. Leadership that only shows up when I've got the title was never leadership — it was positional authority wearing a nicer word.

Next project, I take the role I'm handed, whatever it is, and I make it undeniable. Gracefully. Elegantly. With the greater good and the client's outcome in front of my preferences.

And if I forget again, I've got a guy for that.

## Posting Notes

**The one thing to know:** LinkedIn does not render Markdown, and it does not support multiple inline images inside post text. This file is the canonical, fully-embedded version — great for GitHub, Notion, a personal site, or a newsletter. For LinkedIn itself, the mechanics are different:

1. **Paste the plain-text block** into the composer.
2. **Attach one piece of media.** Pick one — LinkedIn's algorithm favors native video, so uploading the Teague clip natively (rather than linking it) will meaningfully outperform. The Rock GIF as the attached image is the funnier play; the Teague clip is the higher-reach play. If you want both, Teague goes in the post, The Rock goes in your first comment.
3. **Put outbound links in the first comment, not the post.** Link-in-post suppresses reach. Post it, then immediately comment with the clip links.
4. **First two lines are everything.** "I spent the last few months being a worse teammate than I am a professional." is the hook — it sits above the "…see more" fold. Don't bury it.
5. **Timing:** Tue–Thu, 7:30–9:00am in your audience's timezone.
6. **Reply to every comment in the first 90 minutes.** That window drives distribution more than anything else in this file.

**Socializing it across your peer group:**

- Tag nothing and no one in the post itself. Instead, DM 5–8 peers the link with one line: *"wrote something uncomfortable about being a non-lead on a project — curious if you've hit this."* Asking for a reaction to a specific idea gets replies; "check out my post" doesn't.
- The closing question is the engine. Keep it as the last line so it's the thing people are looking at when they decide whether to type.
- If it gets traction, the natural follow-up post is the tactical one: *"Six things I do now when I'm not the lead"* — the bullet list above, expanded, one per paragraph.
- Do not name the project, the client, or the lead. The self-implicating version of this story is the one that builds trust; a version where someone else is identifiable is the one that costs you.

**One honest flag on the source:** the Teague/Fisher clip is verifiably real and widely circulated, but this container's network blocked me from actually playing it, so my summary is reconstructed from coverage of it, not from watching it. Give it a 30-second watch before you post and adjust the retelling if I've got a detail off. The "the NBA is full of stars who still have to find a role" framing is *your* takeaway from the clip, not a direct quote from Teague — which is exactly how it's written above, and how it should stay.
